# Storyline: An Unequal Picture

The central message is that diabetes is more common in some Australian populations than others, and careful comparisons help us understand those differences.

1. **The national picture.** Introduce adult prevalence and survey-detected cases. Then use a separate registration series to distinguish growth in affected-person counts from changes in age-adjusted prevalence. A consistent survey definition provides another view of change. Next question: which adults have higher prevalence?
2. **Age and sex change the picture.** Show the large differences across age groups, then examine sex within those groups. Read point estimates with their uncertainty. Next question: what differences remain when we account for age?
3. **Beyond age alone.** Compare age-adjusted estimates across area disadvantage and remoteness, then examine health profiles among adults with and without diabetes. These are group comparisons and associations, not evidence of individual causes. Next question: how does this appear across states and territories?
4. **Higher rates. Larger counts. Different questions.** Use maps to separate the proportion affected from the number of affected adults. A state with more affected adults does not necessarily have higher prevalence. Next question: how can the definition itself affect the estimate?
5. **How we measure matters.** Show how two tests classify the same survey participants. The alluvial compares classifications, not changes over time. Close by connecting population differences with fair comparison.

The final conclusion is: **Unequal prevalence. Careful comparisons.** National totals describe only part of the picture. Account for age, distinguish counts from percentages, and check the population, period, definition and uncertainty behind each estimate.

The page does not claim that diabetes reacts differently in different people, that observed associations prove causes, or that the registration and survey estimates form one continuous series. It describes how common diabetes is within the populations covered by each source.
