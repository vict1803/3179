01

THE NATIONAL PICTURE

More than a diagnosis.

Some cases emerge through testing.

About 1 in 15 adults had diabetes in 2022–24. This includes people with known diabetes and cases newly detected through the survey.

“Newly diagnosed” here means detected by survey testing. It does not measure every new case arising that year.

Known diabetes 5.6%Newly diagnosed 0.9%Not classified with diabetes 93.5%

Counts and rates tell different stories.

The registered population grew from about 460,000 to 1.31 million between 2000 and 2021.

Age-adjusted prevalence rose earlier, then remained broadly stable from around 2011. A growing count does not imply the same growth in the adjusted rate.

Age-adjusted means allowing for differences in population age. It supports fairer comparisons.

A second view of change.

In the fasting-glucose survey comparison, age-adjusted known diabetes rose from 4.0% to 4.8%.

The newly detected estimate changed from 0.8% to 1.0%. These point estimates alone do not establish a significant increase.

02

WHO IS MOST AFFECTED?

Age changes the picture.

Prevalence is higher at older ages.

About 1.8% of adults aged 18–44 had diabetes, compared with 13.7% of those aged 75 and over.

These are percentages within each age group. They do not describe the share of all diabetes cases belonging to that group.

Look inside the age groups.

Male point estimates exceed female estimates in every age group shown. The male estimate peaks at ages 65–74.

The colour encodes prevalence only. Sex is shown by column position, avoiding a second colour meaning.

Some estimates have large sampling errors. Use the reliability notes in the tooltips; avoid treating every gap as statistically significant.

03

POPULATION DIFFERENCES

Beyond age alone.

A substantial disadvantage gradient.

Age-adjusted prevalence was 10.3% in the most disadvantaged areas and 2.9% in the least disadvantaged areas.

That is around 3.5 times as high. The connected points show the male–female gap within each group.

Groups describe areas, not an individual's income. Group 1 is most disadvantaged; Group 5 is least disadvantaged.

Place also matters.

The combined outer-regional and remote category has the highest point estimate among these three groups: 8.0%, after accounting for age.

Combining these areas can hide important differences. Very remote communities are excluded from this survey comparison.

Health characteristics cluster.

Adults with diabetes also had higher point estimates for these four health characteristics. Age adjustment reduces differences due to population age.

Follow one line across the four axes. Every axis measures a percentage, making the profiles comparable.

These are associations, not proof of cause. The blood-pressure difference was not statistically significant in AIHW's analysis. Click a legend entry to focus on a profile.

04

THE GEOGRAPHICAL PICTURE

Higher rates. Larger counts.
Different questions.

Hover over a region for its estimate. The control highlights all three maps.

Compare rates fairly.

The choropleth uses age-adjusted percentages, rather than raw counts. Larger land areas therefore do not automatically mean more affected people.

South Australia's point estimate is 8.4%; the ACT's is 3.9%. Survey uncertainty means these should not be read as exact rankings.

The map uses an equal-area conic projection suited to Australia. Very remote areas and discrete Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities are outside NHMS coverage.

Now count affected adults.

Circle area represents the estimated number of adults with diabetes. These counts come directly from published survey estimates.

Victoria and New South Wales have large counts. That tells a different story from comparing the proportion affected.

Symbols sit inside the regions they represent; they are not clinic locations or individual cases. NT precision was not published in the source RSE table.

Resize the regions around people.

Western Australia looks large on a conventional map. Here, Victoria becomes larger because its estimated affected population is larger.

Each shape's area represents affected-person counts. Colour adds a different measure: age-adjusted prevalence, on the same scale as the choropleth.

Shapes are uniformly resized and repositioned. Their rough geographic order remains, but distances and adjacency are schematic. NT's estimate excludes very remote populations and has no published precision measure here.

05

READING THE ESTIMATES

How we measure matters.

Two tests can classify adults differently.

HbA1c and fasting glucose measure different aspects of blood sugar. This diagram links the classifications of the same survey participants.

To reveal the smaller groups, the diagram omits the 85.0% classified as “no diabetes” by both methods.

These bands compare classifications, rather than changes over time. Colours show whether the tests agree. Hover over a band, or select a category to follow its connections.

THE TAKEAWAY

A national condition.
An uneven distribution.

Diabetes prevalence varies across age groups, population characteristics and places. Read every estimate alongside its population, period and definition.

Interpret with care

Prevalence measures people living with diabetes. Incidence measures newly arising cases. Survey-detected cases are not an annual incidence series.

The health surveys, registration data, crude percentages and age-adjusted rates are separate measures. They have not been joined into a single time series.

Sex categories follow the source's sex-at-birth reporting. Survey exclusions, missing results and small samples limit coverage and precision.

Tooltips retain reliability information. Blank or suppressed cells are never replaced with zero.
